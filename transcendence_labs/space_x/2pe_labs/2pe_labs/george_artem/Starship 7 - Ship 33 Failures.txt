Starship 7 - Ship 33 Failures - A Narrative

There are dramatic differences between a successful atmospheric re-entry and a catastrophic failure. When an object slams into the atmosphere at 7.8km per second or 17,500 mph, a typical low earth orbit, it hits a wall of air molecules, compressing and superheating them to thousands of degrees. This creates the glowing plasma, the peak heating and friction phase typically lasts on the order of seconds to a couple of minutes depending on the trajectory and speed. 

The intense atmospheric heating phase is pretty short. For a steep, fast re-entry the worst of it can be over in 10-20 seconds as the object decelerates rapidly, shredding most of its kinetic energy.

For reference, telemetry data shows that the Space Shuttle Columbia was traveling around 12,144 mph when it broke apart upon re-entry at around 200,000 feet. The breakup of the heat tiles initiated a chain reaction: aerodynamic forces tore it apart, and the wreckage kept tumbling through the atmosphere. Those pieces, no longer streamlined or protected, were exposed to intense heat and drag for much longer—minutes, not seconds—as they fell.

The official flight plan for the rocket known as Starship 7 consisted of two phases. The first phase, initiated by the supermassive booster that was caught, completed successfully on January 16, 2025, from Starbase, Texas. From there, Ship 33 separated and launched following an eastward suborbital trajectory over the Gulf of Mexico, past Cuba, and toward a planned splashdown in the Indian Ocean, however Ship 33 met an untimely end. 

Liftoff occurred at 4:37 PM CST (22:37 UTC) and telemetry data indicates that at roughly T+8:26, Ship 33 was at an altitude of 146 kilometers (roughly 479,000 feet) traveling at a speed of about 21,000 kph (13,000 mph) when contact with Ship 33 was lost.

T ~ 7:15 was the final nominal trajectory call out indicating that Ship 33 was within reasonably expected flight path boundaries and was experiencing no issues.

T + 7:40 one of six raptor engines shut down, followed by more engine shutdown. This was not part of the scheduled in-space engine re-light procedure, which was scheduled for later in the flight given official statements and goals of testing Ship 33's new Block 2 features.

The Block 2 design included “higher thrust engines,” implying improved performance, possibly through tweaks to the combustion chamber, injector design, or turbopump efficiency. These upgrades aimed to boost thrust and reliability, critical for the Block 2’s stretched propellant tanks, which increased fuel capacity for longer missions but required engines to handle greater loads. The Block 2 ship carried six Raptor engines—three sea-level and three vacuum-optimized (RVacs)—and the in-space engine re-light likely focused on ensuring these could operate seamlessly under ascent and space conditions.

T + 8:26 telemetry freeze with one engine still firing and SpaceX lost contact at 146km altitude. The telemetry loss officially explained as the result of a fire in the aft section, possibly from an oxygen or fuel leak, leading to the initiation of the FTS system and an explosion.

Once the vehicle broke apart, the pieces should have been expected to follow ballistic paths back toward Earth under gravity.
